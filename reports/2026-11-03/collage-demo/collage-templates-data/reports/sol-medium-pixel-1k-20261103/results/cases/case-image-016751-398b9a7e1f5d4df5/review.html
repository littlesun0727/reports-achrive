The arduous and complicated method for turning lapis into usable paint had not yet been discovered—the gemstone was hard and difficult to grind, and unless its impurities were removed, the resulting paint was dull and grayish. The earliest successful examples of paint derived from lapis lazuli have been found on 6th-century Buddhist frescoes in Afghanistan. Its use spread throughout the Middle East and Asia, but the technique for extracting ultramarine did not really arrive in Europe until the 1200s—in Italian it literally means “beyond the sea.” Transportation costs, the difficulty of extraction, and the small amounts of resulting pigment made ultramarine the rarest and most expensive pigment in medieval and Renaissance art. During the High Renaissance, artists such as Titian branched out beyond religious subjects, creating compositions that made the most of ultramarine’s luxurious vibrancy. By the 1800s, scientists were trying to find an alternative and affordable blue, and in 1828, a French chemist developed a synthetic ultramarine that cost only about one-tenth of the price of the natural pigment. Although artists complained that “French ultramarine” lacked the glorious luminosity of the true pigment, by 1870, it was far more widely used.
